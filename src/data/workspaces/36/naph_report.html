Detailed Findings by Standard

1. Page 35 — CTEPH Surgery Waiting Time (All 4 Centres)

Standard: 90% of patients undergoing pulmonary endarterectomy should wait less than four months from diagnosis of chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH).

Centre Status Action Planned
Royal Brompton Referenced Continues local patient tracking; aims to adopt a virtual MDT with Royal Papworth to accelerate surgical decision-making.
Newcastle Dependent Defers to Royal Papworth response — outcome outside local control.
Imperial Dependent Defers to Royal Papworth response.
Sheffield Being Addressed Working directly with Royal Papworth; appointed an additional secretary to reduce administrative delays; has dedicated nursing staff monitoring the pathway; investigating coronary angiography wait times.
Key Insight: Three of four centres defer to Royal Papworth for this standard, making it effectively a centrally-managed benchmark. Only Royal Brompton and Sheffield propose concrete local mitigation steps (virtual MDT adoption, capacity investment). The structural nature of this bottleneck — a single national endarterectomy centre serving referrals from across the country — means individual centre action plans can only do so much without system-wide redesign.

Cross-Cutting Themes

Theme 1: Structural Bottleneck at the National Level

The Page 35 standard appears in every hospital's action plan because pulmonary endarterectomy is delivered through a single national centre at Royal Papworth. This makes the standard fundamentally different from the others — no individual centre can independently guarantee it. The solution requires rethinking the referral-to-MDT pipeline nationally, potentially through the virtual MDT model proposed by Royal Brompton.

Theme 2: Data Quality Is a Recurring Problem

Imperial's discovery that external cardiac catheterisations were not reflected in their database illustrates a broader challenge: when multi-centre care pathways involve procedures at institutions outside the reporting centre, the receiving centre's audit database may not capture them accurately. Quarterly review cadences and proximity-of-entry verification are reasonable mitigations but suggest the data-collection architecture itself needs strengthening.

Theme 3: Staffing Is the Universal Lever

Across all four hospitals, the primary recommended interventions involve adding human resources — consultants, secretaries, dedicated patient-trackers, and nursing monitors. This pattern suggests that under-resourcing rather than protocol deficiency is the principal constraint on meeting these standards.

Theme 4: Early Warning Signs Are Working (Mostly)

Sheffield's ability to report a three-year trend showing progressive decline demonstrates that the NAPH audit framework is functioning as intended: identifying deteriorating performance before crises occur. The remaining question is whether the interventions can close the gap quickly enough.

Limitations and Caveats

  • Limited data scope: These action-plan extracts cover only the standards that each centre flagged as requiring local remediation. Many centres may meet all standards and therefore have fewer entries in their action plans. Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence.
  • No denominator data: The reports provide performance percentages but not the underlying patient numbers, making it impossible to assess statistical significance of changes (e.g., whether Sheffield's 79% represents a clinically meaningful change or random variation around 80%).
  • Self-reported data: Performance figures are self-reported by centres and verified through the NAPH audit process, but the verification methodology (complete data review vs sample-based audit) is not specified here.
  • Time-limited window: Trend data covers only three financial years (2016–17 through 2018–19), limiting our ability to distinguish a genuine secular trend from cyclical variation.
  • Synthesis limitation: This analysis synthesises excerpts extracted from full audit documents. Full interpretations should reference the complete published action plans.

Recommended Next Steps

  1. Monitor Sheffield's 2019–20 results closely. The centre has committed substantial new staffing; the next annual report will reveal whether these investments are translating into improved drug therapy initiation rates. An improvement above 80% would confirm the effectiveness of the approach.
  2. Evaluate the virtual MDT proposal. If Royal Brompton's proposed virtual multidisciplinary team with Royal Papworth proves viable, it could be replicated across other centres to shorten the diagnosis-to-surgery decision pathway system-wide.
  3. Standardise external-procedure capturing. Implement a formal mechanism for referring centres to receive confirmation of externally-performed investigations (catheterisation, angiography) so that NAPH audit databases reflect the true picture of clinical activity.
  4. Expand the analysis to include more centres. Currently covering 4 of the UK's pulmonary hypertension centres. Including data from the remaining centres (notably the Royal Papworth Hospital itself) would enable full benchmarking and reveal whether Sheffield's decline is an outlier or part of a wider pattern.
  5. Calculate statistical confidence intervals. With known denominators, determine whether year-on-year changes are statistically significant or within expected random variation, enabling more precise resource allocation decisions.
