# NAPH 10th Annual Report Analysis: Pulmonary Hypertension Care Quality in Great Britain (2018–19)

## What matters most

- **Eleven of 13 national standards were met at a national level**, demonstrating overall high-quality care delivery across specialist PH centres. The two major gaps are Standard 7 (vasoreactivity study recording: 57% vs. 80% target) and Standard 13 (PEA waiting times: only 11% vs. 90% target).

- **Survival for IPAH patients is strongly predicted by age, sex, and WHO functional class.** Younger women with IPAH without comorbidities achieve ~80% five-year survival, while older men with comorbidities drop below 40% within three years. Age-related decline is dramatic: Quintile 1 (age 18–41) maintains 84% survival at five years, versus just 31% for patients with comorbidities.

- **Pulmonary endarterectomy (PEA) waiting times represent the single largest quality gap** — only 11% of cases meet the 4-month standard nationally. No individual centre exceeded 23%. This is cited across all Local Action Plans as requiring system-level coordination with Royal Papworth Hospital.

- **Drug therapy initiation timing has stagnated.** National Standard 8 (drug therapy within 12 weeks of referral) has hovered around 81% since 2017, well below the 80% target barely. Sheffield declined from 87% to 79%, and Golden Jubilee recorded only 61% in 2018–19. Both centres cite right heart catheterisation throughput as the bottleneck.

- **Local action plan coverage is narrowly focused.** Of the five submitted LAP documents, only four distinct standards are addressed: Standards 3, 5, 8, and 13. Notably, no centre submitted a detailed local action plan covering multiple unmet standards. Newcastle was the only centre addressing diagnosis documentation (Standard 3); Imperial College was the only one addressing cardiac catheterization records (Standard 5).

## Patient populations and survival outcomes

### Overall cohort survival

The longitudinal cohort tracks adult patients whose first referral was on or after 1 April 2009. Across this cohort, cumulative survival drops steadily: approximately 85% at one year, 75% at two years, 60% at three years, 50% at four years, and 45% at five years post-diagnosis. By definition, the survival curves are stopped when the patient count falls below 50, so longer-term estimates carry wider uncertainty.

### Sex differences in survival

The most pronounced survival disparity exists between women and men with IPAH without comorbidities. Women diagnosed in this group maintain an average of 7 years and 77 days to reach median mortality, compared to just 3 years and 279 days for men — a difference of roughly four years. This sex gap persists even though baseline haemodynamic parameters are remarkably similar: mean pulmonary artery pressure averages 52.3 mmHg for women and 49.2 mmHg for men, and WHO functional class III represents 71% of women versus 74% of men.

Patients with comorbidities face dramatically worse survival regardless of sex. For women with comorbid IPAH, cumulative survival reaches 40% by 3.5 years; for men with comorbid IPAH, it reaches 40% by 3 years. The presence of additional cardiac or respiratory disease nearly halves expected survival time.

### Age quintiles reveal steep gradients

Age at diagnosis is perhaps the strongest predictor of survival trajectory. Five age-based quintiles show monotonic declines:

| Quintile | Age Range | Median Age | % Female | 3-Year Survival | 5-Year Survival | Days to 50% Mortality |
|----------|-----------|------------|----------|-----------------|-----------------|----------------------|
| Q1 | 18–41 | 32 | 76% | 90% | 84% | Not reached at 6+ years |
| Q2 | 41–54 | 47 | 72% | 85% | 75% | Not reached at 6+ years |
| Q3 | 54–66 | 60 | 64% | 70% | 46% | 4 years + 127 days |
| Q4 | 66–74 | 70 | 56% | 55% | — | 3 years + 203 days |
| Q5 | 74–91 | 78 | 59% | 46% | — | 2 years + 291 days |
| With comorbidities | 26–90 | — | 53% | 43% | — | 2 years + 218 days |

Patients in the youngest quintile (Q1, median age 32) maintain 84% survival at five years. By contrast, patients with comorbidities — who span all age ranges but tend toward older diagnoses (median age 70) — drop to 25% survival at five years. The age gradient is visible in haemodynamic measurements as well: PVR averages higher in older groups, and six-minute walk distances are shorter.

### WHO functional class predicts near-term outcomes

Among patients with IPAH without comorbidities, WHO functional class at diagnosis provides the best short-term prognostic signal:

- **WHO class II**: 80% survive to 3 years, with data extending to approximately 4 years where survival remains at 75%. Only 103 patients initiated in this category, limiting longer-term estimates.
- **WHO class III**: The largest group (811 patients), showing 72% survival at 3 years, dropping to 43% by 8 years.
- **WHO class IV**: Despite being the most severely symptomatic group (213 patients), 55% survive to 3 years, with 48% at 4 years — better than some might expect, suggesting that severe presentations often lead to aggressive treatment.

For patients with comorbidities, WHO class III shows 50% survival at 3 years, falling to 34% by 4 years. WHO class IV with comorbidities lacks sufficient sample size (only 106 patients) to sustain meaningful survival estimates beyond 2 years.

Several noteworthy patterns emerge from this timeline:

- **NS 6 (WHO class & exercise test)** improved dramatically from 80% in 2015–16 to 98% in 2017–18, then plateaued. Documentation discipline appears largely established.
- **NS 4 (Seen within 30 days)** climbed consistently from 47% to 63%, achieving the milestone of 100% of centres meeting their respective targets in 2018–19 for the first time.
- **NS 11 (Quality of life recorded)** doubled its rate from 78% to 96% within three years, reflecting improved integration of emPHasis-10 scoring into routine clinical workflows.
- **NS 13 (PEA waiting times)** has fluctuated between 11% and 18% with no sustained improvement. The median time from diagnosis to PEA surgery has shortened since 2016, yet structural constraints continue to keep performance far below the 90% target.

## Local Action Plan analysis

### Which standards triggered formal action plans?

Five of the eight specialist PH centres submitted Local Action Plan (LAP) documents for the 10th Annual Report. Together, these documents address four distinct standards:

*Figure 6: Heatmap of Local Action Plan coverage. Every centre addresses Standard 13 (PEA waiting times), reflecting the urgency of this gap. Standards 3, 5, and 8 each appear in only one or two centre LAPs.*

### Key themes in planned actions

**Theme 1: System-level coordination for PEA pathways**

All five LAP documents reference Royal Papworth Hospital's role in pulmonary endarterectomy. The common thread across Sheffield, Royal Brompton, Newcastle, Imperial College, and Golden Jubilee is acknowledgment that improvements require joint efforts between referring centres and the national surgical centre:

- **Sheffield**: Appointed an additional secretary to reduce administrative delays and dedicated nursing staff to monitor the pathway. Examining improvements to coronary angiography waiting times.
- **Royal Brompton**: Continues to track patients locally and audit non-compliant cases. Aims to adopt a proposed virtual MDT with Royal Papworth to shorten decision timelines.
- **Imperial College**: Referenced Royal Papworth response entirely, indicating reliance on the central centre's action planning.

**Theme 2: Staff capacity and dedicated tracking roles**

Two centres explicitly link staffing shortages to Standard 8 failures:

- **Sheffield** reported declining performance (87% → 86% → 79%) and committed to hiring a dedicated tracking member plus appointing an additional consultant in October 2019.
- **Golden Jubilee** aims to increase right heart catheterisation throughput and expresses optimism about meeting the standard by the 11th report cycle.

**Theme 3: Database accuracy corrections**

**Imperial College** identified a documentation error rather than a clinical gap: patients had undergone cardiac catheterization, but it was performed externally and the records were missing from the NAPH database. Their corrective strategy involves quarterly reviews to catch discrepancies closer to the entry date.

**Theme 4: Diagnosis documentation processes**

**Newcastle** (Freeman Hospital) implemented monitoring of new patient activity to ensure diagnoses are recorded within the recognised timeframe and accurately represented in the database.

### Assessment of action plan completeness

The submitted LAP documents exhibit notable gaps. Several centres that underperformed on national standards did not submit standalone LAP documents:

- **Great Ormond Street** (children's centre) did not submit a LAP.
- **Imperial College** referenced the Royal Papworth response for Standard 13 despite being the sole centre to document a corrective action for Standard 5.

This suggests inconsistent engagement with the Local Action Plan process, which may limit the Audit's ability to drive measurable improvement at the centre level.

## Treatment patterns

### Therapy escalation correlates with clinical severity

Patients with IPAH without comorbidities receive more intensive combination therapy than those with comorbidities:

| Group | Mono-therapy | Dual therapy | Triple+ therapy | No therapy |
|-------|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| Females (no comorbidities) | 33% | 52% | 12% | 3% |
| Males (no comorbidities) | 38% | 49% | 9% | 4% |
| Females (with comorbidities) | 55% | 41% | 2% | 2% |
| Males (with comorbidities) | 56% | 42% | 1% | 1% |

Patients with comorbidities receive predominantly mono-therapy (55–56%), likely because underlying cardiac or respiratory conditions complicate PAH-directed treatment decisions. Conversely, 64% of women without comorbidities receive dual or triple therapy, reflecting more straightforward indications for treatment intensification.

### First-line therapy preferences

Nationally, 91% of patients initiate PDE5 inhibitor therapy as first-line treatment, exceeding the 80% target. This exceeds previous years' performance (93% in 2017–18). At the centre level, Imperial College achieves 100%, while Royal Brompton lags at 84%.

## Haemodynamic and functional profile summary

Patients entering the longitudinal cohort present with characteristic haemodynamic profiles consistent with pre-capillary pulmonary hypertension:

- **Mean PAP**: 49–52 mmHg (normal <14 mmHg; PH threshold >25 mmHg)
- **Mean PWP**: 9.7–12.3 mmHg (normal <8 mmHg; indicates pre-capillary pattern)
- **Mean RAP**: 9.4–12.3 mmHg (normal 0–7 mmHg)
- **Mean PVR**: 9.5–12.6 Wood units (normal 2.5–4.2)
- **Six-minute walk distance**: 230–247 metres mean
- **emPHasis-10 score**: 29–32 (scale 0–50, where higher = worse quality of life)
- **Cardiac index**: 2.2 L/min/m² (normal 2.5–4.2, indicating reduced cardiac output)

These values cluster consistently across sex groups for patients without comorbidities. Patients with comorbidities show lower lung function: TLco averages 36% predicted versus 51–56% predicted for those without comorbidities. FEV1 and FVC are similarly reduced in the comorbidity groups.

## Implications and recommended next steps

### Immediate priorities

1. **PEA capacity expansion is the highest-impact intervention needed.** The 79-percentage-point gap between actual performance (11%) and the 90% target for Standard 13 demands coordinated investment in surgical slots at Royal Papworth, streamlined pre-operative assessment pathways, and potentially expanding PEA capability to additional centres.

2. **Drug therapy initiation bottlenecks at Sheffield and Golden Jubilee need targeted resources.** Right heart catheterisation throughput should be increased, as both centres explicitly cite this as the primary constraint. Dedicated case management roles can help track patients through the diagnostic-to-treatment pipeline.

3. **The vasoreactivity study standard (NS 7) requires new documentation protocols.** At 57% vs. an 80% target, this newly introduced standard reveals a systematic recording gap. Centres must determine whether patients are receiving vasoreactivity testing but the results are not being captured in NAPH.

### Longer-term strategic directions

4. **Age-stratified risk communication.** Survival data shows that a 32-year-old woman with IPAH without comorbidities has dramatically different prognosis than a 78-year-old man with comorbid IPAH. PH centres should develop personalised prognosis discussions incorporating these validated predictors.

5. **Integration of comorbidity management into treatment pathways.** Patients with comorbidities consistently underperform on survival metrics and receive less intensive PAH-specific therapy. Multi-disciplinary approaches addressing concurrent cardiac and respiratory conditions could improve outcomes in this growing subpopulation.

6. **Broaden LAP submission coverage.** Only five of eight centres submitted LAP documents, and submissions covered at most two standards each. Mandating broader LAP participation would strengthen the feedback loop between audit findings and centre-level improvement.

### Limitations

- **Scottish data exclusion from survival analysis.** Patients submitted by Golden Jubilee (Scotland's PH centre) are pseudo-nymised and cannot be traced via ONS or National Records of Scotland. Scottish patients are excluded from survival curves and lung function analyses, potentially biasing overall estimates given that Golden Jubilee serves the entire Scottish PH population.

- **Survival curve truncation at n < 50.** Curves are stopped when fewer than 50 patients remain, introducing right-truncation that favours apparent longer survival for younger cohorts (which have larger denominators) relative to older ones.

- **Cross-sectional haemodynamic snapshots.** Characteristics tables derive from measurements within 90 days of diagnosis; longitudinal trajectories during treatment are not captured in this report.

- **Self-selected LAP participation.** Centres may self-select which standards to address in their LAPs, leading to incomplete visibility of local quality challenges.

## Methods

This analysis draws from two primary documents published alongside the NAPH 10th Annual Report (October 2019):

1. **NAPH 10AR - Main Report** (NHS Digital, commissioned by NHS England, 24 October 2019): Contains the full set of 13 National Standards, aggregated performance data by audit year (2015–16 to 2018–19) and by PH centre, patient definitions, and methodology notes.

2. **NAPH 10AR - Supplementary Survival Analysis**: Provides Kaplan-Meier survival curves stratified by sex, age quintiles, and WHO functional class for patients with idiopathic, heritable, and anorexigen-induced PAH (IPAH) with and without comorbidities. Data covers adult patients whose first referral was on or after 1 April 2009 (the longitudinal cohort).

3. **Five Local Action Plan (LAP) documents**: From Sheffield, Royal Brompton, Newcastle, Imperial College, and Golden Jubilee. These contain centre-specific planned actions in response to national standard shortfalls.

Surveillance methods include structured parsing of markdown-formatted tables from source documents, tabulation of key metrics across centres and years, and creation of visualisations using Python (matplotlib/seaborn). Survival curves reproduce data directly from the supplementary report; charts are designed to aid interpretation, not independently calculate hazard statistics.
